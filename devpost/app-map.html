A food check with Pip

Three stops to revisit

This is a reference route, not a claim of a completed interactive code tour.

  1. src/App.tsx — Session and setSession hold the active stage. Repeat, exit, and restart replace the selected food/result; valid edits keep one previous result for comparison.
  2. src/lib/nutrition.ts — calculate uses integer-scaled decimal arithmetic. feedbackFor adds a smaller-portion option when multiple servings cross into the higher band. portionChangeMessage explains edits neutrally.
  3. src/components/Result.tsx — Result puts Pip’s explanation above exact totals, the Carb-ometer, sources, and editable servings.

To change this, start here

  • Foods, labels, and supported barcodes: foods.ts; trace evidence in food-sources.md. Missing verified GI stays unavailable. Oat totals exclude milk/toppings.
  • Food discovery: KnownFoods.tsx reads the same catalog as search. It does not maintain a separate list.
  • Pip’s artwork and expressions: Dinosaur.tsx and local public/dinosaur*.svg. Expressions follow activity, not carb bands. Layout is in styles.css.
  • Source links and serving entry: ServingForm.tsx. Sources open a new tab and retain the app’s active check.
  • Camera selection and decoders: BarcodeScanner.tsx, scanner.ts, and nativeScanner.ts. Native detection is optional; ZXing remains available.

A practice to reuse

Test the risky behavior on the real target device early. Camera permission and a live preview worked on the Fold before UPC recognition did. A separate scanner app reading the same wrapper helped narrow the investigation. Optional native detection, camera selection, and a successful phone retry provided stronger evidence than unit tests alone.

Keep that distinction on another project: scanner lifecycle tests establish cleanup behavior, while actual-device trials establish camera usability. Quaker’s box barcode is evidenced and matched in tests, but a physical Quaker scan remains untested.
